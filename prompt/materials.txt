Confirming in the Middle Path
A Primer for Coaches

I. What Confirming Is — and Is Not

Confirming is not agreement.
Confirming is not advice.
Confirming is not interpretation.
Confirming is not leading.

Confirming is disciplined reflection that accurately mirrors:
- The client's content (It)
- The client's emotional experience (I)
- The relational/systemic implications (We)
- The client's goals and intentions
- The client's stated or implied solutions
- The client's execution commitments (if present)

At its highest level, confirming stimulates reflection without directing it.

It increases the client's self-awareness and system-awareness — which is the foundation of problem solving.

In Middle Path terms: Confirming strengthens the client's ability to Sense and Seek before prematurely Solving.

II. Confirming and the Integral Model

The Integral Model reminds us that all phenomena occur in three interdependent dimensions. In confirming we listen for:
- I: Feelings, values, identity, fears, beliefs
- We: Relationships, trust, culture, power dynamics
- It: Goals, structures, strategy, processes, outcomes

Weak confirming often collapses into only the It dimension:
"So you're missing your targets."

Stronger confirming integrates dimensions:
"You're missing your targets (It), and that's creating doubt about your leadership (I), especially in front of the board (We)."

Masterful confirming helps the client see across dimensions without the coach naming the model.

III. Confirming and the S-Loop

The S-Loop is the backbone of Middle Path problem solving:
- Sense – What is happening?
- Seek – Why is it happening? What do I want?
- Solve – What are my options?
- Start – What will I do?

Confirming mirrors these stages.

Level 1 Confirming → Sensing
This is pure reflection of content, emotion, and immediate experience.
Examples:
- "You reorganized to increase accountability."
- "You're feeling trapped."
- "That comment really stayed with you."
Level 1 builds psychological safety. Without Level 1, deeper work is impossible.

Level 2 Confirming → Seeking
Here confirming integrates content + feeling, meaning, explicit or implied goals, and reframing trap language.
Level 2 helps the client clarify what they want, what matters to them, and where they may be caught in distorted thinking.
Example:
Client: "They're frustrating me."
Level 2 confirmation: "You're unhappy when they act that way."
Notice: Victim language is reframed into personal experience. No interpretation is added. No solution is suggested.
Level 2 is the heart of coaching.

Level 3 Confirming → Solving
Level 3 only occurs if the client proposes a solution. It connects the solution, the belief behind it, and the anticipated result.
Example: "You've decided to freeze hiring because you believe that will stabilize runway without cutting staff."
This clarifies the logic chain and the underlying assumptions. It does not evaluate the logic.

Level 4 Confirming → Starting
Level 4 reflects concrete execution plans, timing, and accountability.
Example: "You're announcing the hiring freeze on Friday and beginning reallocations Monday."
Level 4 strengthens commitment clarity.

IV. Confirming and the Three Traps

The Three Traps distort perception. Confirming helps clients escape them without confrontation.

1. The Knower Trap — certainty without testing.
Client: "I know this will fail."
Confirming: "I hear that you cannot imagine this going the way you want."
The coach removes false certainty, preserves the emotional reality, and reopens curiosity.
Knower Trap is an S-Loop blockage at Sense and Seek.

2. The Victim Trap — focus on uncontrollable elements.
Client: "They're sabotaging me."
Confirming: "You're experiencing their actions as undermining your leadership and you are not sure how to respond."
This restores agency, personalizes experience, and avoids blame.
Victim Trap blocks effective Solving.

3. The Sucker's Choice — false binaries.
Client: "If I slow down we'll lose, if I push hard I'll burn them out."
Confirming: "You want both speed and sustainability, and right now those feel opposed."
This widens the cognitive frame without offering a solution.
Sucker's Choice blocks creative Solving.

V. Confirming and Carkhuff

Carkhuff identified three core helping skills:
1. Responding — accurate empathy (Level 1).
2. Personalizing — helping clients see their role and agency (Level 2). Example: "You notice that when you jump in, the team shuts down."
3. Initiating — only appropriate once understanding is complete.

In Middle Path coaching, Initiating corresponds to later S-Loop stages — and only after robust confirming.

Confirming is therefore not passive. It is developmental.

VI. Confirming as System Intervention

Confirming alters the system. When a CEO hears: "You want speed and energy without sacrificing each other," several things happen:
- The I dimension becomes clearer (values).
- The We dimension softens (less blame).
- The It dimension becomes more precise (desired outcomes).

Confirming increases systemic awareness.

VII. The Discipline of Non-Leading

The most common failure modes:
- Adding advice disguised as confirming ("So you need to slow down.")
- Diagnosing prematurely ("Sounds like a trust issue.")
- Escalating emotion beyond client data.
- Introducing solutions not stated.

High-level confirming refines — it does not add.

If the client did not say it or imply it clearly, it does not belong in confirming.

VIII. The Developmental Arc

A coach develops confirming capacity in stages:
- Beginner: content reflection
- Intermediate: feeling reflection
- Advanced: meaning + goal integration
- Expert: trap reframing + systemic integration

Mastery = integration of I, We, and It, across S-Loop levels, without leading.

IX. Why Confirming Matters in Executive Coaching

Executives are often in the Knower Trap (certainty under pressure), in the Victim Trap (board, market, team), and in Sucker's Choice dilemmas (growth vs culture, speed vs quality).

They are rewarded for decisiveness, not reflection.

Confirming creates cognitive space. It strengthens metacognition, emotional regulation, system awareness, and agency.

Without confirming, coaching collapses into consulting.

X. The Highest Level of Confirming

The highest form of confirming:
- Integrates all three dimensions
- Clarifies goals
- Reframes trap language
- Connects solution to anticipated outcome
- Reflects execution plan if present
- Stimulates reflection
- Introduces nothing new

When done well, the client often says: "Yes — that's exactly it."

That moment indicates the S-Loop has deepened, the Integral Model is being integrated internally, and the Traps are loosening.

<document index="2" title="Middle Path Interviewing Primer (sections 1-6 and 11)">
Middle Path Interviewing Primer
A field guide for experienced Middle Path practitioners who want to keep sharpening Sensing +
Seeking, trap-discipline, and confirming skill across contexts (investor/founder, hiring, 360
interviews, partner diligence, stakeholder discovery).

1) The stance: interviewing as disciplined
sensemaking
Middle Path interviewing is a practice of reality-contact.

Your job is not to be impressive, fast, or right. Your job is to be accurate—and to leave behind a
record that supports sound decisions later. Interviewing is one of the cleanest ways to
strengthen the “Sense” and “Seek” muscles in the S-loop, because it forces you to confront
ambiguity without filling it in with certainty.

Two operating principles:

   1. Everything is data.
       Words, tone, omissions, timing, emotional energy, repeated patterns, and contradictions
       all matter.
   2. All conclusions are provisional.
       You don’t “find the truth.” You build testable hypotheses and identify what evidence
       would strengthen or weaken them.

This stance is how you avoid the Knower Trap as an interviewer.

2) The map: S-Loop applied to
interviewing
Interviewing in the Middle Path is almost entirely Sensing and Seeking.

A) Sensing: gather data using the Integral Model

Sensing is broad, concrete, and descriptive. It’s where you collect what is happening.

   ● It: outcomes, processes, structures, constraints, resources, decision systems
   ● We: relationships, trust, conflict loops, norms, influence networks
   ● I: goals, values, beliefs, fears, identity, capability, stress responses

A common failure mode for experienced leaders is “It-only interviewing” (metrics + execution), or
“I-only interviewing” (psychologizing individuals). Middle Path interviewing uses the Integral
Model as a forcing function: don’t leave a dimension un-sensed just because it’s less
comfortable.

B) Seeking: interview for patterns and drivers (without
solving)
Seeking is where you look for why it’s happening and what’s underneath, while still staying in
inquiry.

You move from:

   ● events → patterns
   ● patterns → plausible drivers
   ● drivers → leverage hypotheses (still not solutions)

Seeking also clarifies:

   ● goals (what “winning” is in this context)
   ● values (what “success” must feel like / honor)
   ● constraints and tradeoffs (including Sucker’s Choices)

Interviewing often fails when interviewers jump from Sensing to Solving (advice, fixes,
judgments). The Middle Path discipline is: stay long enough in Seeking that you can see the
system, not just the story.

3) The Integral Model as a sensing
checklist
Think of the Integral Model as both:

   ● a coverage tool (did I look everywhere?)
   ● and a contamination control tool (did I over-attribute to the person?)

It: outcomes, process, structure (what the system
produces and how)
You are collecting observable facts and system mechanics.

It–Have (results):

   ● What outcomes were expected vs produced?
   ● How do we know? What are the measures?
   ● What changed over time? (trend > snapshot)

It–Do (process):

   ●    How does work actually flow? (not the SOP)
   ●    Where do bottlenecks show up repeatedly?
   ●    How do decisions get made in practice?
   ●    Where does rework happen and why?

It–Know (expertise):

   ● What knowledge is required and where is it concentrated?
   ● What is misunderstood or assumed?

It–Be (structures/conditions):

   ● Incentives, constraints, decision rights, roles, tools
   ● Time horizons and attention structures (cadences, meetings, reviews)

        Key Middle Path move: if someone says “people aren’t accountable,” treat that as
        an It–Be and We issue until proven otherwise. What does the system reward,
        permit, and ignore?

We: relationships, culture, influence (how the human
system coordinates)
The We dimension is where many “mystery failures” actually live.

We–Have (social outcomes):

   ● alignment, trust, cohesion, shared meaning
   ● conflict intensity and recovery speed

We–Do (interaction patterns):

   ● how disagreement happens, how feedback moves
   ● who speaks up, who stays quiet, who gets interrupted
   ● how commitments are requested and granted

We–Know (shared mental models):

   ● what “good” looks like; what “priority” means
   ● shared definitions vs semantic drift

We–Be (norms and identity):

   ● what’s safe to say; what’s punished indirectly
   ● team identity (“we are builders,” “we are scrappy,” “we don’t miss dates”)

        A strong interviewer can turn “culture is broken” into a map of specific repeated
        interaction patterns.

I: individual experience, goals, values, capability (without
psychologizing)
The I dimension is essential—just dangerous when used lazily.

I–Have:

   ● what is the person producing personally?
   ● what commitments do they carry?

I–Do:

   ● what do they do when under pressure? when calm?
   ● how do they make decisions? escalate? avoid?

I–Know:

   ● what do they know that others don’t (or vice versa)?
   ● what skills are strong/weak relative to role demands?

I–Be:

   ● values, identity, fears, loyalties, default narratives
   ● what threatens them? what motivates them?

        The Middle Path discipline: treat I-data as data, not diagnosis. Your job is to
        understand how the person is making meaning and acting—not to label them.

4) Confirming in interviewing
Confirming is the backbone of interviewing quality. It is how you:

   ●   prevent misunderstanding,
   ●   establish rapport,
   ●   keep the interviewee engaged and honest,
   ●   and keep yourself out of the traps.

It is also how you gather better data. People give higher-quality information when they feel
accurately understood.

A) Why confirming matters: trust, data quality, and speed
Experienced interviewers often think confirming “slows things down.” In practice, confirming is
what makes interviews efficient because it:

   ●   reduces backtracking,
   ●   prevents false assumptions,
   ●   decreases defensiveness,
   ●   and increases specificity.

A helpful paradox:
The better you confirm, the less you have to “work” to extract data. The system relaxes and the
truth surfaces.

B) Confirming feeling is not optional
In many high-stakes contexts (founder/investor, candidate selection, 360 interviews),
interviewees manage impressions. They will offer polished It-content while protecting I and We
realities.

Confirming feeling (accurately, without drama) is one of the best ways to:

   ● signal safety,
   ● reduce impression management,
   ● and access more truthful information.

Feeling-confirming is not therapy. It’s precision empathy: naming the emotional experience
that is already present in tone, pacing, and word choice.

Example:

   ● Parrot: “You’re frustrated.”

   ● Skilled: “This sounds like a mix of irritation and disappointment—like you expected better
      from the system.”

The second demonstrates understanding of texture and meaning, not just vocabulary.

C) Confirming and Carl Rogers: congruence, empathy,
unconditional positive regard
Middle Path confirming aligns naturally with Rogers’ three conditions for healthy helping
relationships:

1) Congruence (realness)

Congruence means the interviewer is not performing neutrality while secretly judging,
diagnosing, or steering. People feel incongruence quickly, especially executives.

Congruent confirming:

   ● matches your tone to the seriousness of the client’s experience,
   ● uses plain language,
   ● avoids manipulative “nice” phrasing that hides evaluation.

This doesn’t mean over-sharing. It means your presence feels authentic.

2) Empathy (entering the other’s frame)

Empathy in interviewing is the willingness to inhabit the interviewee’s viewpoint long enough to
understand:

   ●   what they perceive,
   ●   what they care about,
   ●   what they fear,
   ●   and how they are making meaning.

Confirming is the primary behavioral expression of empathy:

   ● “Here’s what I’m hearing.”
   ● “Here’s what that seems to be like for you.”
   ● “Here’s what you’re trying to accomplish.”

3) Unconditional Positive Regard (UPR)

UPR does not mean agreement. It means you hold the person as legitimate and worthy
regardless of what you discover.

In interviewing, UPR shows up as:

   ● curiosity instead of prosecution,
   ● respect even when you suspect distortions,
   ● steady tone when the interviewee reveals something uncomfortable.

UPR is how you get access to honest data about mistakes, conflict, fear, and tradeoffs.

Practical implication:
When you confirm feeling, you are implicitly communicating UPR: “Your experience makes
sense to me.” That reduces defensiveness and increases accuracy.

D) Confirming levels in interviewing (and how to do them
well)
Level 1: Sensing confirming (content and/or feeling)

Goal: accuracy + safety

   ● Reflect content in your own words (no parroting)
   ● Reflect feeling as you infer it (lightly, testably)

Examples:

   ● Content: “So the reorg increased decision latency and created more coordination
      overhead.”
   ● Feeling: “Sounds like you’re carrying a lot of frustration—and maybe some regret.”

Micro-skill: “testable language”

   ● “It sounds like…”
   ● “I’m hearing…”
   ● “Is that close?”

Level 2: Seeking confirming (meaning + goal + trap reframe)

Goal: sensemaking + agency
This is where you integrate:

   ●   what happened (It),
   ●   how it landed (I),
   ●   relational consequences (We),
   ●   and what they want (goal).

This is also where you reframe trap language without correcting them.

Victim Trap reframe:

   ● Interviewee: “There’s no trust.”
   ● Confirming: “So you’re experiencing a breakdown in confidence between specific
      groups, and it’s affecting coordination.”

Knower Trap reframe:

   ● Interviewee: “This will never work.”
   ● Confirming: “You’re having a hard time seeing a path where this succeeds.”

Sucker’s Choice capture:

   ● Interviewee: “Either we move fast or we do it right.”
   ● Confirming: “So it feels like speed and quality are in tension for you right now.”

Important: In interviewing, you log Sucker’s Choices—you do not resolve them.

Level 3: Solving confirming (only if they propose a solution)

Goal: record their reasoning chain

   ● “So you’re planning to do X because you believe it will produce Y.”

You are not endorsing it. You are documenting the logic and assumptions.

Level 4: Starting confirming (only if execution plan is present)

Goal: document commitments and timing

   ● “And you’re beginning that change Monday, with the announcement Friday.”

Again: you’re not coaching them to execute; you’re capturing data.

E) The “non-leading” discipline inside confirming
Confirming becomes leading when it introduces:

   ●   new interpretations not present,
   ●   implied judgments,
   ●   solution direction,
   ●   or emotional escalation.

Rule of thumb:
If the interviewee would be surprised by your confirming, it’s probably leading.

Better: offer the confirming as a test:

   ● “Tell me if I’m off, but I’m hearing…”
   ● “Is it more like X or more like Y?”

F) A practical confirming cadence
A useful rhythm for many interviews:

   1. Ask a Sensing question
   2. Confirm Level 1 (content/feeling)
   3. Ask for specificity (example, timeline, data)
   4. Confirm Level 2 (meaning/goal + trap reframe if needed)
   5. Calibrate confidence and evidence gaps
   6. Move to next area (Integral coverage)

This cadence prevents the interview from becoming either:

   ● a cross-examination, or
   ● a storytime session.

5) Trap discipline (what you do when you
hear them)
A) The interviewer’s Knower Trap (the most dangerous
one)
Common interviewer Knower statements:

   ● “So the problem is…”
   ● “This is clearly a culture issue.”
   ● “I’ve seen this before—what’s happening is…”

These are seductive because they feel efficient. They also contaminate the interview.

Middle Path antidotes:

   ● Treat every conclusion as a hypothesis with a confidence rating
   ● Ask disconfirming questions

   ● Seek alternative explanations

Practices:

   ● “What evidence would contradict this hypothesis?”
   ● “What else could explain it?”
   ● “If I interviewed someone who disagreed, what would they say?”

B) Interviewee Knower Trap: opinions presented as facts
You must convert “verdict language” into “data language.”

Verdict → data conversion prompts:

   ●   “What did you observe?”
   ●   “When did you first notice?”
   ●   “How often does that happen?”
   ●   “Who specifically?”
   ●   “What’s a concrete example from the last month?”

Then confirm with uncertainty language:

   ● “So based on X and Y, you’re concluding Z.”

This preserves their meaning while restoring hypothesis-testing.

C) Interviewee Victim Trap: passive abstractions
Abstractions hide agency and prevent diagnosis.

Common abstractions:

   ●   “There’s a lack of trust.”
   ●   “Communication is broken.”
   ●   “Alignment isn’t happening.”
   ●   “Accountability is missing.”

Your job: resolve to actors + behaviors + context + impact.

   ●   “Who doesn’t trust whom?”
   ●   “What do they do that shows it?”
   ●   “When does it show up most?”
   ●   “What happens as a result?”

Then confirm:

   ● “So when A happens, B responds by doing C, and the impact is D.”

Now you have usable data.

D) Sucker’s Choices: log, don’t solve
In interviewing, Sucker’s Choices are valuable because they reveal:

   ● perceived constraints,
   ● fear structures,
   ● and likely decision biases.

Your action:

   1. Confirm the dilemma neutrally
   2. Capture the statement and the feared consequences
   3. Note where it shows up (contexts, decisions, stakeholders)

You are creating a risk and constraint log for later work.

6) Question banks
Below are field-tested prompts. Experienced interviewers use them selectively; the goal is not to
ask them all, but to avoid blind spots.

A) Sensing questions by dimension
It: outcomes, process, structure

Outcomes

   ● “What are the top 3 outcomes you’re accountable for right now?”
   ● “What outcomes are most at risk? How do you know?”
   ● “What changed over the last 90 days in results?”

Process

   ● “Walk me through the last time this went well—step by step.”
   ● “Now walk me through the last time it went poorly.”
   ● “Where do things get stuck most often?”

Decision system

   ● “What decisions are slowest?”
   ● “Who has authority vs who has influence?”
   ● “Where do decisions get revisited?”

Constraints

   ● “What is non-negotiable here (time/budget/legal/reputation)?”
   ● “Where are you operating with scarce resources?”

We: trust, coordination, culture, influence

Trust map

   ● “Where is trust strongest? Weakest?”
   ● “Who trusts whom least, and why?”
   ● “How do you know trust is weak—what do people do?”

Conflict

   ● “What do disagreements look like here?”
   ● “What topics are safe to challenge? Unsafe?”
   ● “How do you recover after conflict?”

Culture/norms

   ● “What gets rewarded here?”
   ● “What does someone get punished for (even subtly)?”
   ● “What do people do to avoid risk?”

Influence

   ● “Who do people listen to most (regardless of title)?”
   ● “Whose opinion can stop a decision?”

I: goals, values, beliefs, capacity

Goals/optimization

   ● “What are you optimizing for right now?”
   ● “What would make this a win for you?”

Values

   ● “What matters most about how this gets done?”
   ● “What would feel like success even if outcomes are uncertain?”

Beliefs and emotions

   ● “What concerns are most alive for you?”
   ● “What are you most confident about? Least?”
   ● “What would be hardest to accept if it were true?”

Capability and stress response

   ● “What do you do under pressure that helps? Hurts?”
   ● “What skills feel most critical for this role/context?”

B) Seeking questions (patterns, drivers, hypotheses)
Patterns

   ● “Where does this repeat?”
   ● “What’s the recurring loop?”
   ● “What tends to happen right before things go sideways?”

Drivers

   ● “What do you think is driving that pattern?”
   ● “What makes it hard to change?”
   ● “What is the system rewarding that keeps it in place?”

Perspective checks

   ● “What would your critics say?”
   ● “Who has a different interpretation?”
   ● “What do you think they’re protecting?”

Evidence checks

   ● “What evidence supports that?”
   ● “What evidence is missing?”
   ● “How sure are you (0–10)?”

Counterfactuals

   ● “When has the opposite been true?”
   ● “Where does this not happen, and what’s different there?”

11) Advanced pitfalls (for experienced
interviewers)
Pitfall 1: “Pattern arrogance”
You’ve seen it before, so you stop being curious.
Middle Path correction: treat experience as hypothesis-generator, not truth.

Pitfall 2: Over-indexing on articulate storytellers
Confidence and eloquence are not accuracy.
Correction: demand examples, timelines, evidence, disconfirming data.

Pitfall 3: Moralized interviewing
When your tone suggests “wrongness,” data quality drops instantly.
Correction: unconditional positive regard + curiosity.

Pitfall 4: Over-psychologizing (I-collapse)

Attributing system failures to personality is lazy.
Correction: always check We/It structures and incentives.

Pitfall 5: Under-using feeling-confirming
Many “professional” interviewers avoid emotion. That creates distance and defensiveness.
Correction: precise, light feeling-confirming builds safety and improves specificity.

Leadership and the Middle Path: A Comprehensive Guide for Leaders

Introduction to Multi-Dimensional Leadership

As leaders, we often find ourselves caught in complex situations that seem impossible to navigate. The Middle Path framework offers a powerful lens through which to view and address these challenges.

The Three Lenses of Leadership

Philosopher Ken Wilber proposes that any human phenomena can be viewed through three essential lenses. Mastering all three is the hallmark of an integrated leader.

The "I" Dimension - Personal and Subjective
This lens focuses on individuals in any situation. As a leader, constantly ask:
- What are each person's goals, including your own?
- What perspectives do people hold about this situation?
- What concerns drive their behavior?
- What values matter to them in this context?
- Do we have the right people with the right knowledge and skills?

The "We" Dimension - Interpersonal and Cultural
Your output in this dimension is trust, solidarity, and community. Critical questions:
- What relationships are at play in this situation?
- Who influences whom?
- How much trust and alignment exists in the team?
- What cultural factors are shaping behaviors?
- Are we falling into groupthink?
- What cliques or alliances are affecting outcomes?

The "It" Dimension - Objective Systems and Structures
Step back and see the bigger picture of physical and structural systems:
- How do organizational structures impact what's happening?
- What work processes are helping or hindering?
- How do our strategies align with current challenges?
- Are we achieving mission accomplishment and productivity?

These three dimensions are closely linked and interdependent. A symptom in one dimension often has root causes in the others. Use this framework as both a diagnostic tool and a source for intervention ideas.

Recognizing Your Leadership Gaps

A fully integrated leader maintains constant awareness across all three dimensions. However, most of us fail to stay fully integrated under pressure. Common patterns:

One-Dimensional Focus:
- Focusing only on "I" makes you self-centered
- Focusing only on "We" makes you popularity-driven
- Focusing only on "It" makes you mechanistic

Two-Dimensional Focus:
- "I" + "We" without "It" leads to feel-good sessions without results
- "I" + "It" without "We" creates lone wolves who ignore relationships
- "We" + "It" without "I" produces groupthink without individual contribution

Ask yourself: Under stress, where do you tend to go? Where is your comfort zone? More importantly, where is your greatest opportunity for learning?

Understanding the Fundamental Gaps

All human action starts with a gap between a current state and a more desirable future state. In each dimension, you'll encounter specific types of gaps:
- In the "I" Dimension: the gap is between who you are and who you want to become — between your current capabilities and the capabilities needed for success.
- In the "We" Dimension: the gap exists between current relationships and the trust, alignment, and community you need to achieve your goals.
- In the "It" Dimension: the gap is between current results and desired outcomes — between where the organization is and where it needs to be.

The Four Levels of Consciousness: Have, Do, Know, Be

Have - Results and Outcomes. The most visible level. While important, focusing only here won't create lasting change.
Do - Behaviors and Processes. You can see what people do, but changing behavior without deeper work often fails.
Know - Content and Expertise. Knowledge, expertise, and understanding. Many leaders invest heavily here through training and education.
Be - Beliefs, Values, and Attitudes. The foundation of sustained change. Your fundamental beliefs, values, and attitudes drive everything else.

The Critical Insight: Visibility is highest at the Have and Do levels, but leverage for sustained change lies in the less visible but more powerful Know and Be levels.

We've all experienced wanting to change a behavior, knowing how to do it, but failing to follow through. The answer lies at the Be level. What gets in our way are conflicting values, irrational beliefs, ingrained habits, and unchallenged stories we tell ourselves.

The Integral Model: Bringing It All Together

The Integral Model integrates breadth of focus (It, We, I) with depth of focus (Have, Do, Know, Be). It is a comprehensive map for examining any situation from multiple dimensions simultaneously. It helps you:
- Check your awareness across all dimensions
- Diagnose root causes when results aren't meeting expectations
- Understand how different aspects of your organization interconnect
- Make interventions at the right level for lasting impact

For example, if you're not getting the results you want (Have/It), the root cause might lie in your culture (We/Be), which then affects individual behaviors (I/Do) and business processes (It/Do).

The Hero's Journey: Your Path Through Change

Anthropologist Joseph Campbell discovered that nearly every human culture shares archetypal transformation stories, the "Hero's Journey" being the most common. These stories reveal three traps you must face if you're to succeed in your leadership quest.

Trap #1: The Knower Trap - The Blindness of Certainty. The most dangerous trap because you don't even know you're in it. You're absolutely certain you're not missing anything important. You can't perceive the gap because your certainty blinds you.

Trap #2: The Victim Trap - Blaming External Forces. You focus on out-of-control elements rather than what you can influence. You look everywhere except at yourself as the source of the gap.

Trap #3: The Sucker's Choice - False Binary Thinking. This trap convinces you that you must either sacrifice your values to win, or sacrifice winning to maintain honor. You see only binary choices: fight or give in, be tough or nice, be a jerk or a pushover.

The Middle Path: Escaping the Traps

Each trap happens when we take extreme positions in how we think or act. The escape lies in finding the integration of opposites — the Middle Path. This concept of moderation appears in virtually all major philosophical systems, from Buddhism to Aristotle's "Golden Mean."

The Hero's Journey applies to personal and organizational transformation through four stages:
- Sense - Pay Attention. Are you connected to yourself, others, and the environment across I, We, and It? Blindness — "not knowing what you don't know" — keeps leaders grounded before they can take off.
- Seek - Understand. Do you understand why you're stuck and what your goal is? Action without full understanding creates busyness, not progress.
- Solve - Create Options. Have you considered various courses of action, or are you following habit? Do you have a realistic plan? Doing what you've always done will get you the same results you've always gotten.
- Start - Take Action. Are you prepared to act? What keeps you from getting started? Having a plan and taking first steps are not the same thing.

Escaping the Knower Trap: Cultivating Openness

We all see the world through mental models — our beliefs, values, aspirations, and fears. These lenses form from childhood experiences. The goal isn't to eliminate your lenses but to become conscious that what you perceive isn't THE TRUTH, but merely a perspective.

The Two Extremes of the Knower Trap
Arrogant Certainty: believing your truth is THE TRUTH — that your perspective is the only rational one and anyone who disagrees must be irrational. This keeps other viewpoints off the table and inhibits problem-solving.
Deferent Certainty: blindly accepting another's perspective as THE TRUTH without critical examination. This is the overuse of openness that results in "yes-man" syndrome. You inhibit problem-solving by keeping your own point of view off the table.

The Middle Path: Openness
Effectiveness lies in integrating confidence and humility. We call this "Openness" — being open to others' ideas while being open with your own perspectives. The mix isn't the same in every situation. There are times to take a stand and times to give up your stand. As long as these decisions use conscious, rational thought, you remain on the healthy middle path.

A Story of Integrity: A couple brought their son to Gandhi to ask him to help the boy break his habit of eating sweets. Gandhi agreed but asked them to return in three weeks. Three weeks later, Gandhi had a long talk with the boy that seemed to inspire him. As they were leaving, the father asked why they'd waited three weeks. Gandhi responded that three weeks ago, he himself had still been eating sweets. Over that period, he broke his own habit. Only then did he feel he could speak with integrity.

Recognizing the Knower Trap in Yourself
When you're in the Knower Trap, you typically exhibit:
- Fear-based thinking (afraid of being wrong or rejected)
- Judgmental attitude (different equals wrong)
- Other-esteem (your sense of self based on others' perceptions)
- Goal to be "right" and save face, or make others "right" to avoid threat
- Seeing yourself as a finished product that must be perfect

When you're in the Middle Path of Openness, you demonstrate:
- Confidence (you know what you know and aren't afraid to stand by opinions)
- Humility (you know what you don't know; difference is interesting and a source of learning)
- Self-esteem (your sense of self based on ability to learn and grow)
- Goal to be effective and find innovative solutions
- Seeing yourself as a work in progress, constantly improving

The Reminder: When you find yourself in the Knower Trap, remind yourself to "speak truth and seek truth."

Real-World Examples of the Knower Trap
Arrogant Certainty - Semmelweis: In the mid-1800s, Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis discovered that women were dying in childbirth because doctors failed to wash their hands after autopsies. At a time when disease was blamed on "bad air," he proposed that invisible particles transmitted disease. When he implemented handwashing with chlorinated lime, maternal death rates plummeted from 18% to under 2%. Though initially rejected, his insight revealed that changing how we think about causality — not just what we do — can save lives.
Arrogant Certainty in Business - IBM: IBM's arrogant belief that mainframes would remain the only legitimate type of computer nearly destroyed the business. Arrogant certainty inhibits innovation in corporate culture.
Deferent Certainty - Korean Air: At the end of the 1990s Korean Air had more crashes than almost any airline in the world. The problem wasn't old planes or badly trained pilots but a hierarchical cultural legacy of deference to superiors, in cockpits designed to be flown by two equals. In a crash in Guam in bad weather, the pilot made an error and the co-pilot didn't correct him. Once Korean Air recognized the problem was cultural, they fixed it.

The Lesson from Living Companies: Arie de Geus, former head of strategic planning at Royal Dutch Shell, found in "The Living Company" that the most enduring companies treat their enterprises as "living work communities" rather than purely economic machines: people matter more than financial assets.

Your Language Reveals Your Mindset
You can observe when you've fallen out of the middle path by your choice of words. The language of certainty expresses opinions in second person ("you") or third person ("it/this/that"). Semantically, second and third person are appropriate for stating facts, not opinions. When you state opinions in the language of facts, you push others toward equal arrogance or toward deference where they quash their own opinions.
The Solution: Use second and third person only for expressing facts. State your opinions in first person — "I think/feel/perceive," "my perspective is." You can do this without any loss of confidence.

Escaping the Victim Trap: Cultivating Agency

This is a fundamental choice in life: whether to place your attention on factors you control or factors you don't control.

The Two Extremes of the Victim Trap
Blaming Others: absolves you of responsibility and leads to either non-productive passivity or aggressive retaliatory behaviors.
Blaming Yourself (Fatally): blaming aspects of yourself over which you have no control — your genetic heritage or core personality. In claiming "I'm just made this way," you avoid being accountable. If you had no choice, there can be no accountability.

The Emotional Cost of Blame: when blaming ourselves or others, we tend to feel resentment, resignation, powerlessness, fear, envy, and low energy.

The Middle Path: Mutual Accountability
Mutual Accountability means you may hold others accountable for their decisions and actions. But you first hold yourself accountable for your own decisions and actions, thereby creating conditions for change. You can only hold yourself accountable when you believe you have choice and are in control of that choice.

A Poem of Opportunity (Edward Rowland Sill, "Opportunity"):
There spread a cloud of dust along a plain;
And underneath the cloud, or in it, raged
A furious battle, and men yelled, and swords
Shocked upon swords and shields. A prince's banner
Wavered, then staggered backward, hemmed by foes.
A craven hung along the battle's edge,
And thought, "Had I a sword of keener steel—
That blue blade that the king's son bears—but this
Blunt thing!" he snapped and flung it from his hand,
And lowering crept away and left the field.
Then came the king's son, wounded, sore bestead,
And weaponless, and saw the broken sword,
Hilt-buried in the dry and trodden sand,
And ran and snatched it, and with battle shout
Lifted afresh he hewed his enemy down,
And saved a great cause that heroic day.

The Power of Agency: when we shift to the mindset of agency, we experience power, peace, optimism, confidence, hopefulness, abundant energy, creativity, and self-esteem.

The Serenity Prayer captures this: "May God give me the Serenity to accept the things I cannot change, the Courage to change the things I can, and the Wisdom to know the difference between the two."

Agency Drives Execution: Accountability is active and carries the seed of change. Blame promotes helplessness and stagnation. In blame-based cultures, execution suffers because people focus more on avoiding blame and less on solving problems. Accountability creates impetus toward action.

The Reminder: When you catch yourself in the Victim Trap, ask "What's my next move?"

The Power of Language: "I Don't" vs. "I Can't". Research on self-talk shows that the language we use to describe our choices serves as a feedback mechanism that enhances or impedes goal-directed behavior. When the source of the goal is internal, saying "I don't" rather than "I can't" significantly improves follow-through. Psychological empowerment mediates this effect.

Escaping the Sucker's Choice: Cultivating Integrity

The Olympic Ideal: Baron Pierre de Coubertin founded the modern Olympic Games to help build a peaceful and better world by educating young people through sport. The Olympic ideal embodies the integration of winning and honor — competing with integrity, striving for excellence while respecting values.

The Danger of Winning at All Costs: Studies show that pursuing overly ambitious goals can be harmful. When people focus on a goal and failure has high costs, unethical behavior increases. The Sears example: in the 1990s, Sears set a high quota for auto repair sales ($147/hour). Staff overcharged customers and recommended unnecessary repairs. When employees care exclusively about reaching a goal, and bad things can happen if they fail, cheating goes up.

The Two Extremes of the Sucker's Choice
Cutthroat Hypocrisy: the collapse of all values to one: getting what I want. All values become subject to external achievement. People in this mindset often publicly claim noble values while violating them.
Dogmatic Hypocrisy: substituting rigid rules for rational application of values. In business, corporate functions like HR and Legal are sometimes accused of this — "The Department of Business Prevention." It occurs when we claim we want what's best for the company while only protecting our turf.

The Middle Path: Integrity
Can a company "do well by doing good?" Ben and Jerry's openly argues it's possible to stay mission-focused and thrive financially, integrating winning and success.
The middle path for the Sucker's Choice is integrity — the integration of flexibility (finding innovative ways to win) and discipline in living our values (success). In Cutthroat Hypocrisy, we have only one value — winning — and we'll violate any other claimed value to achieve it. In the opposite extreme, we apply values so narrowly and inflexibly that we become self-defeating.

Impact on Sustainability: Lying, cheating, and stealing can get quick results but usually make those results short-term. Integrity creates trust and loyalty in the system, creating conditions for sustained performance.

The Reminder: When you catch yourself in the Sucker's Choice, ask "What would it mean to play to win with honor?"

In business, Sucker's Choice extremes often show up as conflict between sales and legal — sales selling products the company can't deliver, and legal so concerned with risk that no deals can be made.

Using Adversity as Opportunity: Stress is not unhealthy; distress is. Distress occurs when we tax ourselves too far beyond our capabilities. Under-stressing ourselves is equally harmful as we lose capacity through under-use. The way to growth and learning is through alternating periods of challenge and recovery.

Heroes You Admire (exercise): Choose three people or characters you admire — real or fictional, alive or dead. Note the characteristics each has that you admire and an instance in which they displayed those characteristics. This helps you clarify the values you want to embody.

From Mindset to Skillset: Collaborative Problem Solving

As Margaret Wheatley states, effective leadership requires moving from mindset to skillset — from the internal work of cultivating openness, agency, and integrity to the external work of collaborative problem-solving.

Most problem-solving happens in groups, so the medium is conversation. Two types of conversations:
- The Conversation for Understanding: Sensing and Seeking to clearly define the gap.
- The Conversation for Action: Solving and Starting to find solutions and move to implementation.

Critical Success Factor: know which conversation you're in. When part of the group is in sensing/seeking mode while others are in solving/starting mode, it's usually not pretty. The solution is to step back and agree on what conversation you're in.

Building Effective Relationships: I-You, Not I-It
Philosopher Martin Buber proposed two kinds of relationships:
- I-It: one person sees and treats the other as an object rather than a person, with no interest in the other's goals, feelings, or aspirations.
- I-You: each party sees the other as a person fully deserving attention and dignity. This is the only relationship that allows true dialogue.
A third type:
- It-You: equally dysfunctional. You see yourself as not deserving to have opinions or feelings and purely defer to the other.
Of the three, I-You is the only one consistent with openness, accountability, and integrity. It is the healthy Middle Path of relationships and the key to developing trust, alignment, and community.

Research shows expectations significantly impact performance. Leaders who see team members as capable individuals worthy of respect get dramatically different results than those who don't.

Balancing Inquiry and Advocacy
In healthy dialogue, there's a balance between expressing your own point of view and seeking to understand the other's. The Middle Path is wide — the balance needn't be 50/50 in every conversation. When one mode completely takes over, you fall out of dialogue and your chances of success diminish drastically.

Sensing: The Foundation of Problem Solving
Collaborative problem-solving starts with mindfulness. In the busyness of our days we lose connection with the world around us and inside us. Sensing is consciously scanning both exterior and interior worlds to see opportunities, threats, and changes. In a meeting, how consciously do you pay attention to your own mood, assumptions, and mindset; your attitudes toward others; the relationships between people; behaviors, postures, gestures, and expressions; the room setup; and the relation of the topic to processes, structure, goals, and strategies?

Seeking: Understanding Root Causes
Once you've sensed a gap, seek to understand its implications. The Three Steps of Seeking:
1. Create Hypotheses. What could really be going on here? Asking "why" five times is one tool. You can also look for root causes using the It-We-I framework.
2. Test Your Hypotheses. A good test seeks to disprove your hypothesis. Beware of only collecting data that supports your idea.
3. Define What You Really Want. Clearly define both ends of the gap — current and desired states — before moving to solutions.

Finding Your Core Truth
Finding the Path of Integrity in difficult conversations requires self-honesty. Often what you're acting like you want is based in "shadow" values — self-protection, self-promotion, getting your way. The trick is to look to your aspirational values and find the outcome that serves those values.

Productive Advocacy: Getting Your Full Point of View on the Table
Don't make the other person guess about your facts and reasoning. The Four Elements:
- Context: Start with your purpose or desire for the conversation.
- Past: State your facts, your reasoning, your experiences. Own your opinions.
- Present: Share your values and interests. Explain what you really want.
- Future: Propose a plan or request a proposal. Explain the implications you see.
Context expresses what you care about for this conversation; Present expresses what you care about for the larger situation.

Avoiding Dysfunctional Inquiry
- Fake Listening: pretending to listen when you're focused on something else.
- Sneaky Questions: using a question to insert or disguise an opinion without owning it.
- Toxic Inquiry - Loaded Questions: a question carrying an embedded assumption you hope the other will accept without question.

Productive Inquiry: Fully Understanding the Other's Point of View
- Context: What is the other person's intent for this conversation? What is their frame of reference?
- Past: What facts do they have? What experiences? What inferences have they drawn and how are they related to their facts? What is their reasoning?
- Present: What is their Core Truth (what do they really care about)? What are their concerns and deeper interests?
- Future: What implications do they see? What actions do they propose?

Confirming Understanding: Content and Feeling
A key part of inquiry is confirming that you've correctly understood the other person. They communicate through words, sounds, expressions, and body language. Confirm the whole message.
Example: You ask a colleague if he liked your presentation and he answers, "It was good," while looking uncomfortable and not meeting your eyes. Confirming might sound like: "I hear you saying it was good, but you seem uncomfortable about something. Is there some aspect of my presentation that worries you?"
The full meaning of a message includes both what they say and how they feel about it. You can only confirm what the other person has given you:
- If they give you only content, confirm only content (e.g., "You were late with this report").
- If they give you only feeling, confirm only feeling (e.g., "I'm really angry with you").
- Use questions to get the full picture.
Feeling comes through body language and tone, not just words. You can demonstrate empathy by indirectly confirming it: when someone says "You were late with this report!" with a sharp tone and a frown, you might confirm: "You seem angry because I let you down."
Confirming both content and feeling moves you toward resolution more quickly.

Solving: From Ideas to Decisions
We often fail to differentiate between ideas and proposals. Start by brainstorming ideas, move to a smaller number of concrete proposals, and finally use solid decision-making to close.

Brainstorming with Discipline:
1. Set a clear question, based on your conversation for understanding.
2. Set a time and stick to it. Silence often brings out the best ideas.
3. Go for quantity. Don't filter. Capture ideas where everyone can see them.
4. Build on each other's ideas.
5. Don't evaluate. It's OK to clarify, but judging ideas inhibits future ideas.

From Ideas to Proposals: evaluate, combine, and tweak ideas into a small number of concrete suggestions. Signal with language that these are fully packaged proposals.

Decision-Making Methods: know how you'll decide before you get to the decision point. Five ways groups decide:
1. Authority: a single person decides.
2. Minority: a subgroup of experts tasked by the leader decides.
3. Majority: a binding vote at a set threshold.
4. Consensus: everyone is willing to live with the decision. One dissenter can kill it. Allows commitment without full agreement ("disagree and commit").
5. Unanimity: all members fully support the proposal. Does NOT allow "disagree and commit."
Dysfunction lies in either moving to decision without sufficient discussion or getting stuck in endless discussion.

Verbal Aikido: Handling Attacks with Grace
Hold both confidence and humility. Stand calm and centered, leaning neither toward nor away.
1. Listen to the toxic statement. Example: "This report is useless!"
2. Respond by confirming the statement in a non-toxic format: "You're angry with me because the report isn't what you wanted."
3. State your positive intent and begin inquiring: "I want to make this useful for you and I'm sorry it's not. Can you tell me specifically what is missing?"
4. Use dialogue skills to address disagreement.
Stay in Aikido mode until the other is calm enough to enter discussion. You can't force them there. Be patient.

Starting: From Decision to Action
Deciding is not doing. To move from decision to action, you need a plan and commitments for who will take accountability for which tasks.

The Anatomy of a Commitment: a contract freely entered into by two or more parties that answers: Who will do what by when? This requires a clear request and an unequivocal answer.
Making a Clear Request:
- Context: state your intent and make clear you are making a request.
- Past: give necessary facts and background.
- Present: communicate why this is important and give the larger picture.
- Future: make your request: "I ask you to do X by Y. Will you do it?"

Commitments vs. Targets: "I will try" is a commitment to some vague level of effort. A commitment implies a high degree of control over the factors affecting performance and is tantamount to a promise. A target is appropriate when many uncontrollable factors affect performance: "We will attempt to hit this target." Separating them helps you face the reality that we may keep all our commitments and still miss the target.

Accepting Commitments: Can Do and Will Do Factors. When accepting a request, you put your integrity on the line.
Can Do (ability): Do I have the knowledge, skills, and experience? Does the know-how exist somewhere I can get it? Do I have or can I get the resources (time, tools, information) in time? Is my task dependent on others completing theirs?
Will Do (intent): Do I have mental reservations about following through? Does the goal conflict with other goals important to me? Do I care about the goal and/or the person I'm promising?
When making a request, you can ask these questions of the promisee to check commitment.

The Extremes of Starting: Demand vs. Hint
The Demand Extreme: "You must do this." Refusing the other's freedom to say no. You may get compliance, but not commitment.
The Hint Extreme: "I'd like it if you did this." It requires no answer. You may believe you've made a request, and a nod may mean only "I understand what you're saying."
The Middle Path: Clear Requests. "In order to accomplish X, I request that you do Y by Z. Will you do it?" When the person says yes, you have a clear commitment and may hold them accountable — not for your request, but for their promise.

Managing Broken Commitments: the cost of a broken commitment rises the longer the debtor waits to tell the creditor it's at risk. Even when we can't fulfill a commitment (winning), we maintain integrity by honoring it — communicating risk early and mitigating damage.

Making Productive Complaints: you can punish, ignore, or help the other get back into integrity. Steps:
- Context: "I want to understand what happened and get us back on track."
- Past: confirm you both understood the request the same way. Find out what happened.
- Present: explain the impact — not to punish, but to understand.
- Future: make a new request and get an answer. Agree on steps to prevent recurrence.

Making Effective Apologies: a simple "sorry" usually frustrates.
- Context: "I'm not here to make excuses. I want to make this right."
- Past: confirm you made the commitment. Explain what happened.
- Present: inquire about damages. Listen to the other's story. Say you're sorry.
- Future: ask what you can do to make it right and form a new commitment. Agree on steps to prevent recurrence.
The best apology won't satisfy if you consistently fail to keep commitments.

Putting It All Together

A Diagnostic Lens: when you're not getting the results you want, use the Integral Model to ask: Are you focused on all three dimensions (I, We, It)? Are you working at the right level (Have, Do, Know, Be)? Which of the three traps might you be in?

A Path Forward: Sense your gaps with awareness; Seek to understand root causes; Solve by generating options and making decisions; Start by making and keeping clear commitments.

The Three Mindsets of Effective Leadership:
- Openness - Speak truth and seek truth. Balance confidence with humility. Your perspective is valid, but it's not the only valid perspective.
- Agency - What's my next move? Focus on what you can control. Hold yourself accountable first, then hold others accountable.
- Integrity - Play to win with honor. Don't fall into the false choice between winning and living your values.

The Skills of Collaborative Problem-Solving: build I-You relationships. Practice Productive Advocacy and Productive Inquiry (Context, Past, Present, Future), Confirming Understanding (content and feeling), Disciplined Problem-Solving (know which conversation you're in), and Clear Commitments (clear requests, clear answers, honor your word).

Deepening Practice: identify your patterns under stress (which trap, which dimension you favor or neglect); choose one focus area at a time; practice daily; seek feedback on blind spots; reflect weekly; study your heroes; build a support system that holds you accountable.

What's your next move?

The Trust Equation
Why the thing we reach for under suspicion is rarely the thing that repairs it
A Middle Path framework

"I don't care how much you know until I know how much you care." — attributed to Stephen Covey

Almost everyone nods at that line. But notice what it claims: of all the things that might earn your trust — my expertise, my track record, my reliability — the one that gates the rest is whether you believe I care. The research makes this sharper than folk wisdom, and a little uncomfortable, because we consistently behave as if the opposite were true.

Trust as a function, not a feeling

In the Middle Path model we treat trust not as a mood or a personality trait but as a judgment another person makes about us — one that follows a describable structure:

Trust = (Competence × Character × Caring) / Risk

The trust someone extends to me is a function of three C's — Competence, Character, and Caring — divided by the Risk they take on by trusting me. Two features of the equation do real work.

It is multiplicative, not additive. The three C's are not a checklist where strength in one offsets weakness in another. They multiply. If any single C drops to zero — if I am demonstrably incompetent, or visibly self-serving, or you conclude I simply don't care about what you care about — trust collapses regardless of how strong the other two are. A brilliant, principled colleague who you believe is indifferent to your interests does not earn partial trust. He earns none.

Risk sets the bar. The same three C's that are more than sufficient for a low-stakes ask become inadequate when the stakes climb. This is why trust that felt solid can evaporate when the situation changes: nothing about my Competence, Character, or Caring fell — the Risk simply rose, and the C's that were enough before no longer clear the higher bar.

Each C lives in a different dimension

The three C's are not interchangeable because they are not the same kind of thing. Mapped onto the Integral Model, each occupies a different dimension and is proven at a different level of depth, which is why they earn trust on different timelines.

Competence is an It. It lives in the It dimension — skills, resources, knowledge, demonstrable capability — largely a matter of Have and Know. It is the easiest C to demonstrate in a single conversation: I can explain my reasoning, walk you through my strategy, point to my experience, and you can update your estimate in real time. In Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman's widely cited model of organizational trust, this is "ability."

Character is an I, made visible through what you do. It lives in the I dimension at the deepest level, Be. It is who I am when no one is checking and when holding to my commitments will cost me something. Being can only be shown through Doing: you cannot observe my integrity directly; you infer it from how I act when integrity is expensive — telling you the inconvenient truth, challenging you at some risk to myself, keeping a promise that has become costly. In Mayer and colleagues' model this is "integrity." Because it reveals itself only over repeated occasions of personal risk, Character is built by consistent behavior over time; a single conversation can only hint at it.

Caring is a We. It is irreducibly relational: not a property I hold on my own but a question about the space between us — do we value the same things, and do you believe I have your interests at heart? Even with full Competence and Character, you may withhold trust if you sense our goals conflict. This is Mayer and colleagues' "benevolence." A folk maxim, a decade of Middle Path practice, and the dominant academic model of trust converge on the same three pillars: ability, integrity, benevolence; Competence, Character, Caring.

Summary:
- Competence — the It dimension; proven at the level of Have / Know. Demonstrable in a single conversation.
- Character — the I dimension; rooted at the level of Be, expressed through Do. Built over time, under personal risk.
- Caring — the We dimension; a property of the relationship, not the individual. Evidenced through attention.

The paradox: we reach for the wrong C

When trust falters, studies of trust repair are consistent about which C is most often in doubt. It is not Competence and not Character. It is Caring. The suspicion usually lives in the We dimension: "I'm not sure this person is actually on my side."

And here is the trap. When we sense we are not trusted, the overwhelming instinct is to raise our Competence. We explain more. We marshal evidence. We talk. It feels productive, but we are answering a We-dimension doubt with an It-dimension display, solving the wrong term of the equation. Worse, the talking itself often signals the opposite of Caring: a person flooding the conversation with their own reasoning is, by definition, not listening. So we work hard and decrease trust.

This is close to a default. Under the pressure of feeling doubted, retreating to the It dimension is more comfortable than entering the We — analysis is safer than vulnerability. A Middle Path practitioner learns to notice and resist that pull.

The move that actually rebuilds trust

If the broken C is usually Caring, the repair is rarely more talking. It is listening — carefully, and deeply enough that the other person feels it. This is the mechanically correct intervention, and the evidence is robust: a meta-analysis spanning hundreds of studies found that perceived listening — the felt sense of having been heard — is most strongly associated with the quality of the relationship itself. When people feel genuinely listened to, they experience the other as more responsive, lower their defenses, and become more open — the conditions trust requires. You can claim to care; you can only demonstrate it by attending.

This resolves the Covey line. It is a precise statement about the structure of the equation: Caring is the gate, the We dimension is where trust most often breaks, and listening is the one move that speaks directly to it. Competence (the It) can be shown by talking. Caring (the We) can only be shown by listening. When trust is in question, the instinct to prove the first blocks the second.

The practical pattern
- When you feel doubted, your instinct will be to raise your Competence — to explain, justify, and prove. Treat that instinct as a signal, not a strategy.
- Diagnose the term. The doubt is usually in Caring (the We), not Competence (the It). Ask which C is actually in question before you act.
- Make the multiplicative move. One zeroed C collapses the whole product. Restoring the weakest C does more than amplifying a strong one.
- Repair Caring by listening — not as a tactic, but because attention is the only evidence of Caring the other person can actually feel.

The more we want to be trusted, the more we are tempted to perform the dimension we can control on our own — our Competence. But trust is built in the We, and the surest way into the We is to stop proving and start listening.

Leading Self

Personal effectiveness requires the integration of long-term and short-term planning. Long-term planning without short-term leads to poor execution, while short-term planning without long-term ends up in busy-ness without true accomplishment.

THE LONG GAME

The "Long Game" is about seeing the field. It is understanding what results you want to attain (Having) in the various arenas of your life and what values (Being) you want to guide you as you pursue your goals. Only with clear knowledge of these two dimensions can you productively choose the actions (Doing) you will take in the "Short Game."

You can look at effectiveness from three perspectives:
- Work Life - creating impact in the world
- Social Life - forming relationships
- Personal Life - self-actualization
These overlap to a greater or lesser extent for each person, depending on their values, personality, and desires. The work you choose can affect your relationships as well as your personal learning, growth, and sense of accomplishment. Your relationships may affect the work you choose and how you go about it, and can be a source of learning and satisfaction. Your personal goals can influence your choice of work and the people with whom you form relationships.

Work Life questions:
- Of what type of organization (if any) do you want to be a part? Big? Small? Growing? Stable? Having what impact on the world?
- What do you want to do in this organization? Work as an individual contributor? Lead, manage, or mentor others? Be involved in strategy and planning or more in execution?
- Do you want to be customer facing or internally focused?
- What sort of compensation do you want to receive?
- What sort of company culture would be a good fit for you?

Social Life questions (family, community, friends):
- Of what communities do you want to be a part?
- To what degree do you want your work community and other communities to mix?
- What is your ideal family life? How much time do you want to spend with your family, doing what? What about extended family?
- How much time do you want to spend with friends unrelated to family? Doing what?
- What roles do you want to play in your city, town, neighborhood?

Personal Life — the development of the self, in physical, emotional, and intellectual dimensions:
- Physical: How fit do I want to be? In what fitness activities do I want to participate, solo or group? How do I want to eat? How much sleep do I need?
- Emotional: What is the role of love in my life? Do I like emotional highs and lows or being more even-tempered? How do I increase my emotional range? To what degree do I have deep connections with others?
- Intellectual: What is the role of learning in my life, at work and outside it? In what areas of knowledge am I interested? Am I more of an experiential or book learner? What is wisdom to me?

The Tom Sawyer Exercise: In The Adventures of Tom Sawyer, Tom and Huck view their own funeral. Think about whom you would like to speak at your funeral and what you would like them to say. If a funeral feels too macabre, imagine a retirement party.

The Coat of Arms: We aspire to many values, but it helps to choose a small number to keep most present. The coat of arms exercise narrows your values to the four most relevant to you right now.

Ikigai: A Japanese concept whose closest meaning is raison d'être — what one lives for, what makes life worth living. It goes further, to the point where passion can consume you: the artist or inventor absorbed in the work, the gardener whose mind is still among his creation.

THE SHORT GAME

The Short Game is about effectiveness, defined as the integration of efficiency with proper focus: grounding Long Game aspirations in productive action.

Roles: Choose the five roles you currently want to focus on (e.g., manager, parent, partner, friend, community member, learner).

Effective Goals have a minimum of three components:
1. Performance - what will be produced and how. Example: I will create a meeting agenda for the next staff meeting by checking with team members and then using a template.
2. Conditions - constraints, dependencies. Conditions are musts; failing to meet one is failing the goal. Example: I must talk with every team member; every agenda item will have a clear outcome; I must send the agenda at least 2 hours before the meeting.
3. Standards - measures of success, targets. Example: free of typos; out 24 hours before the meeting; addresses the needs of all team members.

Urgent vs. Important (popularized by Stephen Covey): Importance means severe consequences for not getting done. Urgency means something feels like it must be done quickly. Something that draws your attention (a phone ping) isn't necessarily important, yet we often respond to urgent, unimportant requests while putting off important tasks that don't feel urgent.
- Q1 - urgent and important: a customer crisis, a system outage, a high-priority assignment from your supervisor.
- Q2 - important, not urgent: planning, networking, mentoring team members.
- Q3 - urgent, not important: calls, emails you're only cc'd on, meetings you don't need to attend.
- Q4 - neither: most procrastination activities.

Time Charging: Immediately record any activity that comes to you or occurs to you in a place you trust you'll find it and act — on your calendar or a task list.

Weekly Planning (before the week begins):
1. Review your long-term goals.
2. Evaluate the previous week: what went well, what you'd change; revisit accomplishments.
3. Review your roles, asking for each: what are the most important actions I could take this week to have the greatest positive impact?
4. Organize the upcoming week: add the actions to your task list, prioritize, and time charge.

Daily Planning (the night before or first thing in the morning):
1. Update calendar items that have come in since the previous day.
2. Re-prioritize your task list. Note commitments at risk and immediately contact and renegotiate.
3. Block time for Q1s.
4. Block remaining time for Q2s.

THE 12 DUTIES OF MANAGEMENT (self-assessment: rate required skill and current skill, 1-5)
- External Awareness: tracking industry, regional, and company developments that could affect the team.
- Interpretation: keeping people informed about key policies, priorities, issues, and trends and how they apply to the team.
- Representation: presenting, explaining, selling, and defending the team's activities to management, others in the company, clients, and industry contacts.
- Collaboration: liaison with and integration of the team's work with other units, functions, or regions.
- Strategic Planning: deciding on longer-term goals, objectives, priorities, and courses of action.
- Tactical Planning: converting plans to action through short-term objectives, scheduling, and standards.
- Budgeting: preparing, justifying, and administering the team's budget.
- Material Resource Administration: ensuring supplies, equipment, and facilities; overseeing procurement and logistics.
- Human Resource Management: projecting staffing needs; recruiting, selecting, promoting, appraising; identifying, developing, and retaining key talent.
- Supervision: day-to-day guidance and oversight; recognizing performance; improving the bottom of the distribution; concentrating rewards and opportunities on the strong majority and top performers.
- Team Performance Management: tracking the status of team activities, identifying problems, and taking corrective action.
- Program Evaluation: assessing whether goals are achieved and how effective team operations are, to find ways to improve.

Leading 1-1
(Compiled from: Leading One-on-One, OPICS and TOPPS, Job Analysis Template, Understanding Human Performance)

PART 1: DELEGATION

Effective delegation uses the It-We-I framework:
- It = the task
- We = the relationship and degree of freedom
- I = the performer

The "It" of delegation: OPICS — five components of task clarity
1. OUTCOMES — the end state. What gets produced (products or services). Focuses the performer and reduces confusion. Example: a post-mortem report on a project. Key question: What is the end state that will be achieved?
2. PROCESS — the method. How the outcome should be produced. Match detail to the performer's experience; if you are less expert than the performer, leave the method to them. Example: lead a collaborative process; the team writes; you edit and give feedback. (You delegate responsibility, not necessarily all the work.) Key question: What major duties, methods, or strategies?
3. INPUTS — the resources. People, materials, tools, and information. More detail for newer performers; experienced performers can request inputs. Example: project team members, key stakeholders, tracking data, previous after-action reports. Key question: What people, information, materials/tools are needed?
4. CONDITIONS — the non-negotiables. Constraints, dependencies, parameters — "must haves": deadlines, budget limits, coordination requirements, values/ethics. Examples: include an executive summary; follow the client's branding guidelines; meet the end-of-month deadline. Key question: What are the musts and must-nots?
5. STANDARDS — the measures of success. Quantity, quality, cost, timeliness; may include stretch goals. Distinct from Conditions: you can miss a standard and still succeed. Examples: complete the report five days before the hard deadline; create insights that improve the next project. Key questions: How do we know it's done right? How do we know it's done well?

The "I" of delegation: choosing the right performer
Three screening questions:
- It: What level of task expertise is required?
- We: What level of interpersonal competence is required?
- I: What personal characteristics are required (initiative, patience, etc.)?

Five levels of performance (Carkhuff scale):
- Level 1 – Detractor: actively works against the goal. Reconsider delegating.
- Level 2 – Observer: follows explicit instructions; no initiative. Needs comprehensive direction and frequent check-ins.
- Level 3 – Participant: actively engages; can work from a general description; needs some guidance.
- Level 4 – Contributor: generates solutions independently from a clear problem statement; minimal oversight.
- Level 5 – Leader: needs only a pain-point. Defines problems, creates solutions, executes, and develops others.

DiSC work style matching:
- D (Dominance) — results-oriented; bold action, quick decisions
- I (Influence) — people-oriented; teamwork, communication, enthusiasm
- S (Steadiness) — process-oriented; reliability, consistency, patience
- C (Conscientiousness) — detail-oriented; precision, analysis, careful planning

The "We" of delegation: degrees of freedom
- Level 1 – Wait to Be Told: no action without explicit instruction (Detractors, crises).
- Level 2 – Ask: identify options; get approval before acting (Observers).
- Level 3 – Recommend: analyze, develop recommendations, present for approval (Participants).
- Level 4 – Act Then Report: take action; inform afterward (Contributors).
- Level 5 – Act Autonomously: full authority; report only on significant issues (Leaders).

Calibrating detail to performer level:
- Detractor — consider not delegating at all.
- Observer — delegate your solution with a full plan.
- Participant — provide a general description; they develop the plan.
- Contributor — give a clear problem statement; they generate solutions.
- Leader — point to the pain-point; they handle the rest.

The commitment conversation (Context-Past-Present-Future):
- Context — signal this is a formal request, not just an idea. "I want to talk with you about taking ownership of X. I'm making a formal request."
- Past — background: the challenge, root causes, data, prior attempts. Inquire about the performer's knowledge and experience.
- Present — connect to purpose: organizational goals and/or the performer's development goals. Elicit their reactions and interest.
- Future — create the contract. Ask for active commitment: "Are you willing to take this on?" Clarify any fuzzy OPICS elements.

PART 2: COACHING

Preparing yourself (It-We-I self-check):
- It — What's the business context? Do I have the facts? Any assumptions?
- We — What is the trust level? How do I want to use this to build the relationship?
- I — What is my mood? What preconceptions am I bringing?

Three foundations (Carl Rogers):
1. Congruence — authenticity; behave consistently with your values.
2. Empathy — genuinely understand the performer's perspective.
3. Acceptance — unconditional positive regard for the person's worth and potential.

Dual purpose of every coaching conversation:
- Short-term: solve the immediate business problem.
- Long-term: build the performer's capability.

Directive–facilitative continuum
More directive (faster results, less learning):
- Give Solutions — "Do it like this." For time-critical or safety issues, or very inexperienced performers.
- Teach — walk through steps and reasoning together. For new skills.
- Give Feedback — share observations on outcome vs. expectation and why. After performance, for course-correction.
More facilitative (slower, deeper learning):
- Specific Questions — "Who was at the meeting?" "What did you do next?" To help reconstruct and analyze experience.
- General Questions — "How did you see the problem?" To develop reflective and problem-solving capability.
- Active Listening — "So I hear that you decided X for reasons A, B, C." To build trust and support emotional processing.

Deconstructive feedback (avoiding the Knower Trap)
Knower Trap (avoid): "I'm right and you're wrong." "I have the answer and you don't."
Middle Path feedback (practice): "We both may be right or wrong in parts." "We will likely both learn from each other." "I may have ideas you haven't thought of — and vice versa."
Using Context-Past-Present-Future for feedback:
- Context — set a collaborative tone: "We're here to figure out what happened and get better — not to prove who is smartest."
- Past — reconstruct reality. Separate facts from inferences.
- Present — identify what matters. What do we each care about in It, We, I?
- Future — create action plans. What will we do? What can we learn?

Coaching through the 4S framework

Stage 1 — SENSING (What actually happened?)
- It questions: What was the outcome? What steps did you take? What tools/info did you use?
- We questions: Who was involved? What did they say? Who influenced things offstage?
- I questions: What do you know about each person? What did they care about? What assumptions did you carry in?
Knower Trap cues: opinions stated as facts, gaps in the story. Questions to expose it: "Is that a fact or an interpretation?" "What exactly did they say — word for word?" "What other explanations could there be?" "What might you be missing?"

Stage 2 — SEEKING (What is the root cause of the gap?)
- Form hypotheses: "Why do you think that happened?" "What's beneath that?" "What are three possible root causes?"
- Test hypotheses: "How could you test that?" "What evidence would prove you right or wrong?" "Who could give you a different perspective?"
- Define the goal: "What does success look like?" "What values do you want to honor?"
Victim Trap cues: language of blame; focus on others' failures or external constraints. Questions to escape it: "What was in your control?" "What could you have done differently?" "How are you contributing to this situation?"

Stage 3 — SOLVING (What should be done?)
Sucker's Choice cue: binary thinking — "I had to sacrifice values to get the result." Questions to escape it: "It sounds like you see this as an either-or. Is that right?" "What would it mean to honor both your values and achieve the result?" "What's a third option you haven't considered?"
- Brainstorming: discourage evaluation during generation. "What's the opposite of your first idea?" "What would [person they admire] do?"
- Developing proposals: "Which ideas seem most promising?" "What are the pros and cons of each approach?"
- Decision-making: "What criteria are most important?" "What information do you still need?"

Stage 4 — STARTING (How do we make it concrete?)
Help the performer create a fully articulated goal (conditions + standards), build a plan with steps and milestones, test commitment (check for doubts or mental reservations), and identify needed support.
Key questions: "What's your first step?" "When will you take it?" "What obstacles might you face — and how will you overcome them?" "On a scale of 1–10, how confident are you that you'll follow through?" "What would make it a 10?"

PART 3: CAREER DEVELOPMENT COACHING

Three factors of sustained happiness:
1. Meaning — Is my work consistent with my values? What impact do I want to have?
2. Control — Do I have the right amount of autonomy and choice?
3. Mastery — Am I getting more skilled at something I care about?

Ikigai — five elements to explore:
1. Achievements — What are you most proud of? What patterns appear in your best moments?
2. Knowledge — What topics fascinate you? What do you read voluntarily?
3. Skills — What do you love doing regardless of whether you're "good" at it? What activities make time fly?
4. Self-Image — How do you introduce yourself? What roles are most central to your identity?
5. Personality — What energizes you? What drains you? What environments suit you best?

PART 4: JOB ANALYSIS

A job analysis captures the full picture of a role using It-We-I:
- Outcomes (It): What deliverables are produced? What is the end state of the work?
- Process / Duties (It): What major duties, methods, strategies? What steps or milestones lead to accomplishing the task?
- Inputs (It): What people, information, materials/tools are needed?
- Conditions (It): What are the musts and must-nots? Values and constraints. Time, resources, dependencies.
- Standards (It): How do we know it's done right (quality)? How do we know it's done well (excellence/stretch)?
- Interpersonal requirements (We): Who does this role interact with and how often? What groups? What level of interpersonal skill? What can this person do without permission? Who supports this person?
- Individual requirements (I): knowledge; technical and problem-solving skills; interpersonal skills (self-awareness, other-awareness, engagement); communication skills (written, verbal, conflict, influence); attitudes, behaviors, and other personal characteristics; physical and intellectual capabilities.
- Context: company vision, mission, values; company goals; competencies required; criteria for success; labor market and industry status and trends.

PART 5: UNDERSTANDING HUMAN PERFORMANCE

Performance has internal (I) and external (It/We) sources. No single model is complete — use these as tools to organize thinking, not as truth.

Five levels applied across dimensions:
- Level 1 (Detractor): Problem-solving: does not recognize problems; misrepresents them. Sensing/Seeking: does not find root causes. Interpersonal: listens passively; largely unaware of social dynamics. Communication: expresses incompletely; creates conflict. Standards: well below standard.
- Level 2 (Observer): Problem-solving: recognizes problems in isolation; surface-level analysis. Interpersonal: general grasp of others but doesn't check inferences. Communication: expresses when asked; doesn't adjust style; shuts down in conflict. Standards: follows instructions but won't exceed targets.
- Level 3 (Participant): Problem-solving: finds root causes within a system context; links issues to own behavior. Interpersonal: curious; tests inferences; hears beyond words. Communication: expresses actively; adjusts style to audience; holds ground in conflict; separates facts from opinions. Standards: engages actively in understanding quality, quantity, and timelines.
- Level 4 (Contributor): Problem-solving: improves on existing solutions; creates complete, high-quality plans. Interpersonal: correctly perceives mental states and motivations; sees the system. Communication: articulates clearly; builds strong data-based arguments; manages conflict skillfully in the moment. Standards: creates own targets with manager input; consistently exceeds relevant targets.
- Level 5 (Leader): Problem-solving: makes significant contributions; creates paradigm shifts. Process: sees that processes constantly adapt; creates new value-add processes; challenges non-useful ones. Communication: inspires, motivates, influences; adapts style easily; gets to the root of conflict; builds community. Standards: exceeds targets in organizationally relevant ways; often develops others.

The freedom ladder matches the five degrees of freedom in delegation (Wait / Ask / Recommend / Act-Report / Act). At a decision point, ask what level of freedom is appropriate for this performer.

Incentives: extrinsic (compensation, recognition) and intrinsic (meaning, mastery, control). You control behavior-shaping conditions, not thoughts directly.

Strategies for working with performers:
- Level 2 (Observer): detailed instructions; frequent check-ins.
- Level 3 (Participant): discuss best ways to do things; delegate a solution in principle.
- Level 4 (Contributor): delegate the problem with clear parameters.
- Level 5 (Leader): co-create the mission; turn them loose.

Leading Teams

Human systems are organic, not mechanical
For a long time we've been taught to think of human systems as mechanical — gather the parts, bolt them together, turn it on. People still call a team a "well oiled machine." The metaphor is not only inaccurate, it's harmful. Human systems are biological and organismic, governed by chaos theory and non-linear mathematics. A human system has more in common with a pond than a machine.
- Mechanical thinking: single operator outside the system; single-function parts; manage by numbers.
- Organismic thinking: distributed intelligence; interchangeability; manage by mission.

How to Manage by Mission
- Pay attention to initial conditions — the beginning of the year, the quarter, a meeting. Use these moments to create alignment to mission and intent.
- Establish a Team Charter that has meaning to the team. Talk about it. Use it as a touch point often. Be an example and actively reward examples.
- Involve others in developing strategy. Review and adjust regularly.
- Push tactics and decision making down as far as possible; emphasize adaptability and agility.
- Mission first and numbers second.
- Separate targets and commitments. Always ensure targets have a rationale. Always ensure commitments are freely given.
- Train and coach on collaborative problem solving and systems thinking.

What makes a team
Two essential elements: (1) a common goal or purpose — lack of clarity here is a common dysfunction; (2) interdependency — the group cannot achieve the goal without relying on each other.
Types of team suit different leadership:
- Basketball team: very high interdependence, fluid roles, lots of interaction. Many project teams work this way.
- Track team: individuals compete in their own events and results aggregate. Some sales teams with separate territories fit this.
- Baseball team: sometimes together (fielding), sometimes individual (batting). Most senior teams work this way.

Creating a Team Charter
1. Identify stakeholders and why they think we exist. If there is a team sponsor, discuss this with them.
2. Create a statement of purpose and goals. What is winning (goals)? What does winning serve (purpose)?
3. Create Ground Rules — principles that guide behavior as we pursue goals. Some teams start with values; ground rules are then behavioral expressions of the values.
4. Define roles. For what is each person accountable? What is the extent of each member's authority?
5. Define team processes, at minimum: rhythm of business (when we meet; regular milestones like goal setting, performance reviews, postmortems); communication (who communicates what, to whom, when, how); decision-making (how important decisions are made, who has authority for what).

Sample Middle Path operating agreements:
1. Pay attention to the system — I, We, It
2. Understanding before action
3. Check all inferences
4. Open with your view and open to others'
5. Focus on what you can control
6. Make clear requests and give clear commitments
7. Define both Winning and Success
8. Optimize for the whole

John Wooden, the UCLA basketball coach who won ten NCAA championships in twelve years (seven in a row, 88 consecutive wins), was known for short, simple messages to his players, including his "Pyramid of Success," often aimed at success in life as well as basketball. His thinking has had lasting influence on leadership and teams.

Tools for the team
- The Integral Model helps in Sensing (what to pay attention to and measure), Seeking (diagnosing root causes of performance gaps in Have), and Solving (ideas for solutions with systemic effect rather than suppressing a symptom).
- DiSC helps understand the team's psychodynamics. As in Leading 1:1, chart team members based on your assessment of where they stand on the two axes.
- Tuckman's stages of team development (Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing) — see below.

Meetings
Meetings are where most team interaction happens — and one of the things people complain about most.

Five essential meeting roles (one person may play several):
- Facilitator — owns the process of the meeting.
- Content Owner — often the manager or project leader; default decision-maker; decides scope and purpose. When the team leader is both Facilitator and Content Owner, the temptation is to use power to over-control the meeting, discouraging participation.
- Time Keeper — tracks time and pushes the team to make conscious tradeoffs when a discussion runs over.
- Recorder — captures, at minimum, decisions and commitments.
- Participant — everyone who contributes; distinguished from a "spectator."

The Facilitator's job is an effective process. It helps if the facilitator is neutral about content so they don't use the process to influence decisions. A leader who facilitates faces a choice: stay neutral and facilitate well, or engage in content and risk steering the process toward preconceived outcomes. Basic roles: create a safe environment, control pace, enable participation, keep on track, ensure solid closure. Knowing DiSC styles helps manage meeting dynamics.

Three stages of facilitating: effective preparation (participants arrive ready to engage), effective conducting (the right types and amounts of participation), effective follow-up (participants follow through).

Preparing: get clear on what you want from the meeting, using OPICS. Example (a team planning Dorothy's trip to the Emerald City): Outputs — a plan for getting there. Process — Sensing, Seeking, Solving. Inputs — people (Dorothy, Tin Man, Lion, Scarecrow, a Munchkin representative), materials (maps, charts, pens), information (a list of people knowledgeable about Oz). Conditions — finish by 4:00; must have consensus; the plan may not go through the Dark Forest. Standards — the plan is complete and fully documented; everyone feels the process was fair.

Agendas: essential sections are Outcomes (tangible results of the meeting), Times (forces you to consider how much fits), and Items with Purpose (how will we know when we're done?). The purpose of any item is some form of Sensing, Seeking, Solving, or Starting. A typical agenda: Frame (objectives, ground rules, agenda, time — confirm/adjust); decision items (with method, e.g., consensus); inform items (present, clarify/confirm); solution items (status, clarify/confirm, brainstorm); Close (review objectives, action items, keep/stop/start).

Engagement: inform people of the meeting's purpose and how to prepare; engage their interest (what's in it for them); involve them in setting objectives or agenda items. This also tells you who really needs to be there. Consider whether the room setup matches your purpose, whether you have the tools you need, and participants' comfort.

Meeting flow — Framing: check-in, objectives, agenda, ground rules, logistics, roles. Executing: Sense, Seek, Solve, Start. Check-out: actions/decisions, results, process.

Check-in aligns the human and technical dimensions of the meeting, lets people become present, and surfaces conflicting expectations. It can take ten seconds per person. Minimum questions: How are you showing up? (mindset) What outcomes do you want from this meeting? (aspirations) Set a time limit per person; optionally use a talking object.

Every topic includes some part of the 4 S's:
- Sensing topics: report-outs, sharing experiences or data.
- Seeking topics: looking for patterns in data, root cause analysis.
- Solving topics: generating options, narrowing, creating decision criteria, deciding.
- Starting topics: creating plans, agreeing on next steps.

The Sensing conversation gathers data, usually starting from an unsatisfactory result or action, described in concrete, tangible terms. Use It, We, I to look comprehensively:
- It: When and where did it occur? What data do we have about outcomes? Was there clarity around goals? Did structure and role clarity play a part? What processes were in play and how did they work?
- We: What are the social relationships between the people involved? Could anyone be influenced by people we aren't aware of? What is happening in the actual dialogue? How was this affected by culture?
- I: Who said and did what, when, where? What are my own biases or assumptions about the people or situation?

When moving to solutions, a common pitfall is that people don't move through the stages together. A facilitator makes the stages explicit and keeps the team on the same page.

Decision making: the five methods from Leadership and the Middle Path — Authority, Minority, Majority, Consensus (everyone willing to support; "disagree and commit" is OK; one holdout means no consensus), Unanimity (all actively agree; "disagree and commit" is not OK). Good decisions in meetings require: enough Sensing and Seeking to be solving the right thing; exploring ideas and proposals rather than the first idea; knowing you're in a decision conversation; knowing how you'll decide; agreeing on criteria and their weights.

Starting often happens in a separate meeting from deciding. Complex solutions may need full project planning; simple ones move quickly to closing commitments.

Check-out covers: What commitments were made? Did we produce the outcomes we planned? Could we have run the process more effectively?

INTEGRAL TEAM ASSESSMENT
Rate each item from Too Little, through Just Right, to Too Much.
- The It: focus on long-term strategy; focus on short-term execution; focus on change; focus on stability; focus on growth; focus on efficiency; focus on speed; focus on quality; goals are ambitious; goals are achievable; roles are distinct; roles are flexible; the team has defined processes; members follow processes. Overall grade on getting things done (A+ to F).
- The We: members act independently; act collaboratively; challenge each other; support each other; listen to each other; advocate their own positions; the team discusses decisions; moves to closure on decisions; focuses on group accountability; focuses on individual accountability; is diverse in how it thinks about the business; is uniform in how it thinks about the business; members are given room to be themselves; are encouraged to conform to standards. How aligned is the team in a common purpose?
- The I: members are confident in their abilities; know their limitations; hold themselves accountable; hold others accountable; are flexible in taking risks to achieve goals; are disciplined in operating within the team's values; carry their own weight; ask for help; feel stretched; feel comfortable; have deep expertise; have broad expertise. Do you have the right people on the team? (7 = dream team; 1 = severe gaps)

STAKEHOLDER ANALYSIS
1. Identify stakeholders — internal (managers, employees, shareholders), external (customers, suppliers, communities, regulators), and project-specific (team members, investors, contractors).
2. Classify them. Power/Interest grid: high power, high interest — key players needing significant attention; high power, low interest — keep satisfied; low power, high interest — keep informed and engaged; low power, low interest — monitor. Or the Salience model: power, legitimacy, and urgency; those high on all three are most salient.
3. Assess needs and expectations: their interest, current opinion (supportive, neutral, opposing), expectations, perceived risks, and communication preferences — explicit and implicit concerns.
4. Prioritize by potential impact.
5. Develop a strategy for each key group: inform, consult, involve, collaborate, or monitor.
6. Communicate in two directions, tailored to each stakeholder. Assign a team member as the "keeper" of a stakeholder where there's an existing relationship.
7. Monitor and adjust as needs and influence change.

TEAM DEVELOPMENT (Tuckman)
Bruce Tuckman's 1965 research proposed four stages of small-group development. Each stage has two closely integrated aspects: the interpersonal (group structure) and the task behaviors.

Stage 1 — Forming: the group is coming together and testing norms and the boundaries of the task.
Members may feel excited (eager for a new undertaking), anxious (about what the team will accomplish, working in a team, working with strangers), and dependent (uneasy relying on others, unsure what they can contribute).
Issues: testing authority (balancing the desire to test authority with dependence on it), inclusion (fitting in and meeting expectations), and trust (in teammates, leader, and process). Teams here usually make little progress on their task. These issues must be addressed openly and honestly.
Leaders can help by adding structure (focused meetings, clear tasking, defined roles), encouraging learning (members asking about each other's expertise and working styles, e.g., using DiSC), and balancing participation (encouraging everyone and discouraging dominant behavior, especially early).

Stage 2 — Storming: members realize how much work lies ahead and that the task is more complicated than anticipated, and see a gap between early hopes and reality.
Members may feel incompetent and confused, frustrated by team requirements and the time things take, and negative toward the leader and other members. Watch for symptoms of the Victim Trap.
Issues: power (who can make it happen?), control (who will be in charge?), conflict (how do we resolve differences of opinion?). Much of this is unavoidable — storming is part of the natural evolution of teams. Use Middle Path skills to shift from conflict (ego-based) to disagreement (idea/evidence-based).
Leaders can help by facilitating dialogue (encouraging members to state ideas and feelings constructively), helping with decision-making (how the group will decide and solve problems), and providing support (resources, reaffirming purpose and capability, running interference with outside groups).

Stage 3 — Norming: members get used to working together, help rather than compete, and begin acting like a team. Watch for the Sucker's Choice: in trying to agree, members may fall into binary thinking without considering integrative solutions.
Members may feel open to constructive feedback, accepted by the team, and comfortable as task anxieties subside.
Issues: team harmony (trust, respect, support), shared responsibility, building confidence.
Leaders can help by backing off (delegating more, letting the team run meetings and make decisions — which takes conscious effort to let go), expressing their own ideas (now that the group can hear them, to keep it out of groupthink), and challenging the team (harder assignments, such as reaching consensus independently on a high-stakes issue).

Stage 4 — Performing: members are comfortable with each other and the project; the team is an effective working unit whose concern is success.
Members may feel appreciative of each other's strengths, accepting of each other's weaknesses, satisfied with progress, confident as a team, and optimistic.
Issues: goal accomplishment and maintaining momentum. The leader's main task is to help the team develop group maintenance skills.
Leaders can help by suggesting new goals as the original task nears completion, testing assumptions (comfort can breed complacency and the Knower Trap — question norms and ground rules), and developing self-assessment processes for individual and team performance.

It may take four to six months for a team to perform well. Size, mix of personalities, meeting frequency, visibility of the task, and the leader's experience all affect progress. Virtually all teams eventually reach performing.

Cycling through the stages: teams don't stay in performing indefinitely. Significant changes — a new leader, a change in purpose or processes — throw the team back into forming. The leader's job is to help the team cycle back to performing effectively, not quickly; forcing the pace can leave the team stuck.

Human Systems: Leading from the Top and Leading from the Middle
(Workshops on human systems dynamics, based on Barry Oshry's work — "Seeing Systems" and "Leading Systems" — which focuses on the We and I dimensions of the human system.)

Contradictions and double binds
Organizational life regularly presents contradictions — usually not on purpose, but through inattention, or because those at the top are receiving contradictions themselves. They are felt most acutely by people stuck in the "middle." Examples:
- Follow the process, but make an exception for me.
- Be committed to the decision, but we won't involve you.
- Being committed by tops to things we know we can't complete.
- If I ask for help you take over, but if I don't ask I can't get guidance.
- Here are your OKRs (without asking if they're achievable).
- Bring up problems, but don't be negative.
- Your OKRs are important, but do this thing that isn't your OKR (and for which you'll get no credit at review time).
- Be honest with us, but tell us you can do everything.

Faced with a double bind, we risk falling into the three traps:
- Knower Trap: we assume the intentions of others without testing. We accept stories we hear without challenging them. We become blind to what "is" because we're certain of what we "know."
- Victim Trap: we focus on the uncontrollable (others' actions) rather than on our response to them. We look to blame rather than striving for mutual accountability.
- Sucker's Choice: we lock onto binary options — fight or flight, stay or leave, be nice or be brutal.

The fundamental idea: human systems take on predictable forms, and people in those systems begin to act in predictable ways that can lead to system dysfunction.

Three kinds of system relationships:
- Top–Bottom: between those with decision-making authority and those tasked to execute decisions.
- End–Middle–End: vertical (execs – middle management – front line) or horizontal (vendor – procurement – user, or teammate – me – teammate).
- Customer–Provider: obvious (buyer–seller) or less obvious (student–teacher, app developer–platform developer).

System blindness
- Spatial blindness: failing to grasp big-picture connections — mistaking the part for the whole, like the blind people describing an elephant. Includes scale blindness: missing the big picture while focused on details, and vice versa.
- Temporal blindness: focus on the immediate, ignorance of the past, and neglect of the future — failing to learn how we got here and to plan ahead.
- Relationship blindness: locking into "us versus them," forgetting that each of us plays many roles in interlocking systems. It feeds one-step thinking, where simplistic "solutions" replace old problems with new ones.
- Process blindness: overlooking how we interact — e.g., not noticing that my words offend you and make it hard for you to listen.

Because we are blind to the system, when things happen we make up a story about it, evaluate others as malicious, insensitive, or incompetent, take it personally, react (get mad, get even, withdraw), lose focus, and reject partnership as impossible. And the dance begins:
1. We see others act in ways we don't like and attribute negative intention.
2. We react by attacking, demanding, withdrawing, avoiding, withholding.
3. The other sees our actions as unjust reactions to their well-intended actions.
4. They react by attacking, demanding, withdrawing, avoiding, withholding.
5. We say "Aha! I knew they were stupid and/or evil..."

Breakdowns: predictable conditions and experiences
- Tops — condition: overload — experience: burdened
- Bottoms — condition: disregard — experience: oppressed
- Middles — condition: crunch — experience: torn
- Customers — condition: neglect — experience: righteously screwed
- Providers — condition: put upon — experience: unfairly judged

Top Overload. The predictable response is to suck all responsibility up to ourselves and away from others. It is a world of unmanageable complications; of feeling alone and unsupported; of worrying about what we aren't doing and what we aren't doing well enough; of an ever-growing agenda; of fearing we're letting down the many who depend on us; of fighting fires; of making decisions because someone must, unsure they're right. Our options seem limited to working longer, working harder, searching for better support, or resigning.
Traps: Knower — "I must be perceived as having the answers," "I must look on top of it all." Victim — "Middles aren't carrying my messages," "Bottoms are sabotaging my initiatives because they don't like change." Sucker's Choice — "I either control everything or dump everything on the Middles."

Middle Crunch. The predictable response is to slide into the middle of other people's issues and conflicts and make them our own. It is a world of being torn between others, pushed and pulled, with both sides questioning our loyalty; of losing independence of thought and action; of feeling responsible for problems between others and never doing enough; of confusion, weakness, uncertainty, and little positive feedback, until we begin to question our own competence. Our options seem limited to aligning with Tops, aligning with Bottoms, bureaucratizing ourselves so no one can reach us, or trying to be evenhanded and burning out.
Traps: Knower — "It won't work," "My peers don't care." Victim — "They keep putting me in the middle," "They keep blaming me for everything." Sucker's Choice — "I either side with Tops or Bottoms."

Bottom Disregard. The predictable response is to hold the "higher-ups" responsible for what's wrong. It is a world where our lives and the organization's are in others' hands and they're making a mess of it; where we're hooked on what "they" do and don't do; where we feel angry, disappointed, betrayed, ignored, and mistreated. Our options seem limited to accepting our lot, suffering in silence, complaining, resisting, or rebelling.
Traps: Knower — "I'm the only one who really understands," "That initiative doesn't make sense." Victim — "They don't care about the impact on me," "There's nothing I can do about it." Sucker's Choice — "I either rant and rave or just suck it up and do what I'm told."

Put Upon Provider. The predictable response is to distance ourselves from the customer, delivering what we decided we promised and avoiding any attempt to reinterpret or renegotiate the deal. It is a world where the customer always asks for more, is never satisfied, never could describe what they wanted, and calls us greedy. Our options seem limited to suffering in silence, refusing contact, giving in to every demand, or complaining.
Traps: Knower — "They're greedy, they want something for nothing," "They don't know what they want." Victim — "They should give me better info," "They should understand I have a business to run." Sucker's Choice — "I screw them or they screw me."

Customer Neglect. The predictable response is to stand aloof from the delivery process and hold the provider responsible. It is a world of entitlement, constant frustration and disappointment, promises made and broken, and being the righteous, blameless victim of unresponsive providers. Our options seem limited to suffering in silence, finding another provider, complaining, or settling.
Traps: Knower — "They just want my money," "They don't care about quality." Victim — "They should keep their promises," "They should know what I want." Sucker's Choice — "I screw them or they screw me."

Door A: the familiar scenarios
- Middles feel alone and isolated, with no support group, uninformed and easily surprised, competitive, evaluated, and see others as dangerous. The result is alienation, an "I" mentality, and diffusion (or clinging).
- Bottoms feel closeness and solidarity, but those who disagree with the majority feel vulnerable and the majority feels threatened by disagreement. The result is "them vs. us," hidden or denied internal disagreements, feeling unseen by management, seeing lots of problems in the system, and needing to protect ourselves.
- Tops feel overwhelmed by complications and inputs, with too much to do, responsible for everything and at risk of personal failure. The result is divided responsibilities, turfism, things falling between the cracks, defensiveness and blame, resisting incursions by other Tops, sabotage, buffers and isolation.
- Customers feel disappointment, too small to matter, outrage, frustration, anxiety, low expectations, and resignation. The result is products and services not delivered as wanted, broken promises, feedback disappearing into a black hole, and always dealing with the wrong people.

Door B: new frames and new actions
For Tops — individually: create responsibility; do what it takes so people feel responsible for their own well-being and security, their world in the organization, and the organization's health. Together: create a powerful vision for your work; make yourselves interchangeable and rotate responsibilities where possible; commit to a coaching relationship with another Top; share high-quality information with each other and the organization; stick to decisions only you can make, make them quickly, and inform others completely.
"In the presence of top overload, my business as a top is not to suck it up and shoulder all of the responsibility... My business is to create responsibility." — Barry Oshry
For Middles — individually: lead when you can (make decisions, exercise judgment, take responsibility for the consequences, don't ask permission, maintain independence of thought and action); don't be a sewer pipe (you're closer to the situation — recognize garbage for garbage, protect others and yourself from it); coach (show you understand, empower others to solve their own problems, provide a framework, teach skills, give feedback); facilitate (bring people together and make their interaction productive). Together: get good at integrating information, making meaning and decisions, and dispersing them; meet regularly as a group with absolute commitment; develop a compelling purpose; keep Tops informed; work the tough issues and produce decisions; act, and ask permission later; hold each other accountable for knowing all parts of the business.
For Bottoms — individually: do whatever it takes to be responsible for your part of the organization; take back responsibility for your own safety and well-being; educate yourself about the business; don't waste energy blaming or asking higher-ups to fix it; be persistent; respect yourself and others. Together: create a strong mission; encourage individual differences; pursue diverse strategies to do the work better and protect your interests; develop individuals' unique skills; stay linked to Middles and Tops and keep them informed.
For Customers: don't hand over total responsibility for providing the right products and services; enable providers to deliver quality; don't stay aloof and complain — get into the middle of the process; avoid intermediaries and stay close to your producer; do whatever makes it easy for the process to work.
Effects: Middles become informed, stronger, supported, and better able to lead. Bottoms get the information, direction, and support they need and a picture of the whole system. Tops get consistent information, can move initiatives uniformly through the system, and are freed up to do Top business. Customers get smoother coordination, a synergy of interests with the organization, and confidence in delivery.

Managing by mission (for Tops): get out of the mechanical mindset — human systems are organic and governed by chaos theory. Managing by the numbers makes them heavy, dogmatic, and bureaucratic. Pay attention to initial conditions; establish a meaningful vision, mission, and values and use them often; involve others in strategy; push tactics and decisions down; mission first and numbers second; separate targets and commitments (targets have a rationale, commitments are freely given); train and coach on collaborative problem solving and systems thinking. Organizations that move through the 4S cycle faster and more effectively than competitors survive and thrive. Tops run the cycle at the macro level and enable their direct reports to run it at the next level, so problem solving happens at the lowest effective level.

LEADING FROM THE MIDDLE

What happens to Middles: the work is hectic and under constant pressure — putting out fires and cleaning up after others. The greater others' difficulty, the greater the pressure on Middles, who are pulled to be responsive to everyone.
How Middles are seen: hardworking, responsible, well-intentioned — and weak, wishy-washy, reactive, confused, with no mind of their own, powerless, uninformed, incompetent, fractionated, and failing to deliver.
Complaints about Middles — from Tops: can't get initiatives through, can't get consistent information up, no entrepreneurs, no risk taking. From Bottoms: not getting information, direction, and support; no visible added value; Middles can't get their act together. From Customers: a stumbling block, antagonistic, controlling, out of touch.
Familiar responses: try to please everyone (burn out, take failure personally); align with Tops (become more of a Top, more rules and control); align with Bottoms (become a super-radical); withhold from everyone (create buffers, respond to no one).

Enhancing the power of the system as a whole: the Middle position offers a unique opportunity to influence the patterns of interaction between others — to move from less functional to more functional interactions by raising dilemmas, naming difficult issues, and identifying the underlying issues that must be discussed. Lead when you can; stop the garbage; show others what you see (show you understand, empower them to solve their own problems, offer a new mindset and language about systems, teach productive dialogue, give feedback); facilitate others' interactions (bring people together to learn about each other and their issues, ask provocative questions, seek to understand divergent views).

Leadership as enabling others' problem solving. One definition of leadership from Leadership and the Middle Path is "enabling the problem solving of others." From the middle you can see where others are stuck and help them move:
- Enable Sensing: make sure both sides separate facts from opinions and get their full data and experience on the table. Look for the Knower Trap.
- Enable Seeking: listen for untested hypotheses and offer ways to test them; keep people focused on what they can reasonably control. Look for the Victim Trap.
- Enable Solving: help others find solutions that meet the needs of multiple parties, and help them define values before solutions.
- Enable Starting: encourage clear requests and productive accountability for themselves and others.
From the middle you have a unique view of the whole system. Use the Integral Model to help others explore systemic root causes and systemic solutions.

Getting sucked back into the middle: others collude by holding us fully responsible for resolving their issues, and we buy it, letting our self-esteem and success rest on how well we resolve their issues. What would it take to stay out of the middle and offer service in other ways?

The Choice: facing conditions that don't change — complex situations Tops are responsible for, negative situations Bottoms believe higher-ups should fix, conflicts that draw Middles in, delivery systems that fail Customers — we stand between two doors. Door A leads to the familiar scenarios: burdened, oppressed, torn, screwed. Door B offers a new frame, new actions, and the possibility of new results.

The Double Bind
*A Middle Path note on a human-systems pattern*

Where the idea comes from

The term "double bind" comes from the anthropologist and systems thinker Gregory Bateson and his colleagues (Don Jackson, Jay Haley, and John Weakland), who introduced it in 1956 in their study of communication in families. Their original claim, that double binds cause serious mental illness, has not held up, and the concept is not used here in any clinical sense. What has lasted is the communication pattern itself, which shows up everywhere people depend on each other. Barry Oshry's work on human systems describes the same structure and its symptoms in organizations without using the term. The Middle Path combines the two: Bateson's name for the pattern and Oshry's picture of how systems produce it.

What it is

A double bind is a pattern, not a single bad message. It has three parts:

1. Two messages that can't both be met. Usually from someone with power over you, or from the system you're in. Satisfying one means failing the other.
2. The contradiction can't be discussed. Pointing it out feels risky, disloyal, or naïve, so people work around it in silence.
3. There's no easy exit. The relationship matters too much, or leaving costs too much, to simply walk away.

Because it repeats, people stop seeing it as a contradiction and start seeing it as "how things work here."

What it looks like at work

- "My door is always open. Bring me solutions, not problems." People who have a problem but no solution freeze.
- "Take risks, but don't fail."
- "Own this decision, but run everything by me."
- A new leader reports to the CEO on paper (It), while everyone knows a founder actually makes the calls (We).
- "We value candor," while the last person who was candid got sidelined.

Seen through the Integral Model, a double bind often lives in the gap between dimensions: the structure or policy (It) says one thing, and the norms and power (We) say another. Or what a leader says they value (espoused) differs from what they reward (enacted).

Symptoms

People caught in a double bind tend to:

- freeze, wait, or delay ("let it cook until it's perfect")
- go around the official channel, influencing indirectly
- hide disagreement and say yes in the room
- feel confused, then resentful, then resigned

The traps follow quickly. Knower: "they obviously don't really want to hear it." Victim: "there's nothing I can do." Sucker's Choice: "either I comply with this message or with that one."

The person sending the bind is usually acting rationally

Double binds are rarely deliberate. The sender is often caught between pressures themselves, or doing what the system has rewarded. Blaming them misses the pattern, and so does blaming yourself. It is a property of the system.

The way out: make it discussable

The bind holds only as long as it can't be talked about. The Middle Path move is to talk about the pattern itself:

- Name the pattern, not the person: "I'm hearing two messages and I don't know how to meet both."
- Stay curious, and assume there may be a logic that makes both messages fit, one you can't see yet.
- Use Context, Past, Present, Future: state your intent (to help, not to accuse), give specific examples, say what you care about, and ask for help.
- Remember agency: anyone who sees the bind can raise it. It doesn't have to be the person with the most power.

How the coach uses this

When a client describes being stuck between messages that can't both be satisfied, the coach can help them notice the pattern ("Are you being asked for two things that can't both happen?"). It can map where the contradiction lives across I, We, and It, keep the focus on the system rather than on blaming the sender, and, if the client wants, help them plan a conversation that makes it discussable. It is also worth asking, gently, whether the client might be sending a double bind to their own people. Leaders often are, without knowing it.
